Three ways to subtract the
background from a cell-frame timelapse.

Every fluorescence frame in the experiment carries the same uneven illumination — bright in the centre, dim at the edges — superimposed on the cells we actually care about. Three different methods recover the underlying field. They disagree on where the background comes from, how to handle the cells sitting on top of it, and whether a single static map is enough.

At a glance

The three approaches, in one sentence each.

Each method aims to produce a per-pixel background map that can be subtracted from any frame. They differ in where they look, what they assume about the underlying illumination, and how aggressively they handle drift over time.

01
Polynomial fit

Sample & fit

Snap a 25×25 grid of probes onto guaranteed cell-free spots, average the local luminosity at each, then fit a smooth 2D quadratic surface through them.

The background is whatever low-order polynomial best explains the readings at clean locations.

Average & blur

The background is the temporally-stacked frame, blurred enough that cells dissolve into context.

Calibrate & anchor

Approach 01

Polynomial fit on a clearance-snapped sample grid.

Lay down a regular grid of probe points; nudge each one to the nearest spot that has never been covered by a cell in any frame; measure the local mean luminosity; then fit a degree-2 polynomial surface to those measurements. The fitted surface is the background.
Pipeline · Approach 01 · polynomial fit
Algorithm
  1. Build a clearance map. Take 15 evenly-spaced frames per channel, OR all the cell masks together, distance-transform the inverse → every pixel knows how far it is from the nearest pixel a cell has ever touched.
  2. Snap a grid to clean ground. Place a 25×25 grid; for each grid point search a 180 px window for the pixel with maximum clearance; keep it only if clearance ≥ 40 px.
  3. Sample the local mean. At each surviving point, average the luminosity inside a 30 px disc, averaged across 10 frames spanning the timelapse.
  4. Fit the surface. Solve a least-squares system for the 6 coefficients of a degree-2 polynomial in normalised coords (x − W/2)/(W/2), (y − H/2)/(H/2).
  5. Subtract. Render the polynomial to a full-res map, shift so the minimum is zero, and subtract from every frame.

A = poly_design(xn, yn, deg=2)
coefs = np.linalg.lstsq(A, sample_means)[0]
bg = sum(coefs[k] * XF**i * YF**j
         for k,(i,j) in enumerate(...))
bg -= bg.min()  # edge → 0

Where it shines

  • Most aggressive enforcement of "samples are guaranteed cell-free" — uses union of all masks, not just one.
  • Polynomial constraint is a strong regulariser; outliers can't bend the surface much.
  • Produces a smooth, mathematically-described background that won't introduce high-frequency artifacts.

Where it strains

  • Degree-2 can't capture asymmetric vignetting, dust spots, or any non-quadratic structure.
  • Static across time — can't follow lamp drift, focus shifts, or photobleaching.
  • Needs enough cell-free area to support 25×25 sampling; degrades on confluent fields.
Approach 02

Mask-aware Gaussian smoothing of the time-averaged frame.

Stack a strided sample of frames into one mean image, mask out the cells visible in the first frame, then run a wide Gaussian smooth that uses the mask as a weight — so the blur both flattens the background and interpolates over the cell-shaped holes in a single pass.
Pipeline · Approach 02 · smoothed average
Algorithm
  1. Stack frames into a mean. Take every 20th frame in the timelapse and average them per-pixel into one image. Cells that move appear as faint smears; static cells survive as ghost residue.
  2. Mask the first-frame cells. Read the cell segmentation for frame 0 only; invert it to get a binary background mask M.
  3. Mask-aware Gaussian. Compute two big blurs — numerator G_σ(I·M) and denominator G_σ(M). Their ratio is a smooth background that has been filled in over the masked regions by their neighbours' values.
  4. Zero the baseline. Subtract the global minimum so the dimmest corner sits at zero.
  5. Subtract. The same map is applied to every frame.
Parameters
BG_CFG
smooth_sigma60Gaussian σ in pixels

# core estimator (per channel)
avg_img = mean(frames[::stride], axis=0)
M = ~first_mask  # True where bg
num = gaussian_filter(avg_img * M, σ)
den = gaussian_filter(M.astype(f32), σ)
bg  = num / np.maximum(den, 1e-6)
bg -= bg.min()

Where it shines

  • No shape prior — captures arbitrary illumination patterns including off-centre vignetting and gradient bands.
  • The temporal average naturally suppresses moving cells; what survives is mostly genuine background plus first-frame static cells, which the mask handles.
  • Single-pass, fast to compute once.

Where it strains

  • Static cells absent from the first-frame mask leave residual structure in the bg map.
  • Static across time — same drift problem as A1.
  • Choice of σ is a tightrope: too small and cell residues survive; too large and the bg can't follow real spatial structure.
Approach 03

Calibration reference, anchored per-frame by ROI offset.

Use a separately-acquired blank-fluid image (here: 2% FBS medium at the matching exposure) as the spatial template for the background. For every cell-frame in the timelapse, compute a single scalar offset from a small cell-free corner ROI and add it to the reference before subtracting.
Pipeline · Approach 03 · reference + per-frame offset
Algorithm
  1. Pick a calibration reference. From a separately-acquired panel of blank-fluid images (rows = fluid, columns = exposure), select one matched to the experimental imaging conditions. Resize to match the cell-frame dimensions.
  2. Define a cell-free ROI. A disc in a region of the field that you trust to never have cells — here a 150 px radius at 92% / 92% of the image (bottom-right corner).
  3. Measure the reference's ROI mean. A single scalar μ_ref.
  4. For every frame: measure μ_frame = mean(frame[ROI]); compute Δ = μ_frame − μ_ref; build shifted_bg = bg_ref + Δ.
  5. Subtract. corrected = frame − shifted_bg. The spatial pattern is fixed (from the reference); the level adapts every frame.

Where it shines

  • The only one of the three that adapts to per-frame illumination drift — lamp warm-up, perfusion changes, photobleaching of the medium.
  • Minimal assumptions about cell coverage; needs only one trusted clean ROI.
  • Spatial template comes from a clean exposure with no cell residue at all.

Where it strains

  • Reference must match the experimental conditions (fluid, exposure, optical path) for the spatial pattern to be valid.
  • A single scalar offset assumes the drift is uniform across the field — if illumination changes shape over time, this can't track it.
  • The "trust this corner" assumption fails if cells ever migrate into the ROI.

The trade-offs, in one table.

No single approach dominates — each picks a different combination of priors, robustness assumptions, and computational complexity. What follows isn't a verdict, it's a checklist for matching the method to the data.

A useful frame: A1 and A2 ask “what does the background look like, given this data?” — they're inference-from-the-data methods, with different priors (polynomial vs. smooth). A3 asks “what does the background look like, given a calibration shot?” — an external prior on shape, with only the level inferred from the data. The first pair handles unusual spatial structure; the third handles unusual temporal behaviour.
