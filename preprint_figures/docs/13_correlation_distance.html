Correlation vs distance

For every cell pair, is response similarity (Pearson r) related to spatial proximity? Tests whether responses are organized in local neighborhoods or distributed independently across the field.

How to read this figure

  1. Look at the slope of the linear fit.
  2. Note the R² and p-value in the legend.
  3. Compare per-channel scatter to the pooled.
  4. Trust low R² fits less, even with low p.
Purpose

Are nearby cells more correlated?

If cells respond as locally-coupled groups (gap junctions, paracrine signaling, shared microenvironment), nearby cells should be more correlated than distant ones — a negative slope of r vs distance. If responses are independent or driven by a global stimulus, the slope is flat. A positive slope is unusual and suggests systematic non-spatial structure (e.g. an imaging gradient).

Inputs

What it reads from state

  • state["corrected_lum"][exp][ch] — converted to a DataFrame with rows = cells, columns = frame indices via lum_dict_to_df().
  • state["traj_by_channel"][exp][ch] — per-cell (x, y) coordinates per frame.
  • state["frame_counts"][exp][ch] — total frame count for the channel.
Method

From traces to a scatter point per pair

  1. Mean cell positions. For each cell, average all (x, y) pairs across frames where both are defined. Cells with no valid coords are dropped.
  2. Filter to cells with both luminosity and position. Need ≥ 2 cells; otherwise skip the channel ("insufficient data").
  3. Pairwise correlation — both Pearson and Spearman. Build pearson_mat = pd.DataFrame(mat).T.corr(method="pearson") and spearman_mat = ...corr(method="spearman") on the full corrected time series. Each panel layout draws Pearson on the top row and Spearman on the bottom row.
  4. Pairwise distance. scipy.pdist(positions, metric='euclidean') → N × N symmetric distance matrix in pixels.
  5. Upper triangle. Take indices np.triu_indices(N, k=1) to extract N(N−1)/2 unique pairs.
  6. Linear regression. scipy.stats.linregress(distance, correlation) per subset → slope, intercept, Pearson r of the fit, p-value, std-err. Compute R² = r² and a fitted-line confidence band: ±3 × SEM at each x — captures ~99.7% of fitted-line variation under repeated sampling.
  7. Plot. Scatter (alpha ≈ 0.30, small dots) + fitted line (linewidth ~2) + confidence band (alpha 0.10).
Responder-pair split

Splitting pairs by responder status

Within each panel, every cell pair is classified using the per-channel responder mask (compute_responder_thresholds(...) + the standard peak-|dF/F0| comparison — same recipe as response_violins.py's responder gate). The three subsets get distinct colours and independent regression lines:

RR — Responder × Responder

Both members of the pair pass the responder threshold. Plotted in red (#d62728). Tight regression here means responder cells share temporal structure regardless of distance.

NN — Non × Non

Both fail the responder threshold. Plotted in gray (#7f7f7f). Acts as the noise floor; expect a flat slope close to zero.

RN — Mixed

One responder, one non-responder. Plotted in green (#2ca02c). Comparing RN to RR/NN tells you whether responder ↔ non-responder pairs sit between the two cleaner subsets.

Subsets with n < 3 are silently skipped (no line drawn). When compute_responder_thresholds returns nothing for a channel — e.g. on an experiment without a usable responder threshold — the panel falls back to a single-cloud scatter with one regression line.

What the cell biology says

Expected trends · interpretation

Negative slope (locally coupled)

r decreases with distance. Suggests gap-junction coupling, calcium waves, or shared local microenvironment. Biologically plausible for confluent monolayers like NRK and PC3. Check magnitude: a slope of −0.001 r/px over a ~500 px field implies r changes by ~0.5 across the field — sizeable.

Flat slope (independent)

r ~ constant regardless of distance. Cells respond independently or to a global stimulus. R² will be low and p-value high. The most common outcome for these experiments.

Positive slope (anti-spatial)

Distant cells more correlated than nearby ones. Atypical and usually an artifact: imaging gradient (focus, illumination), cell-type heterogeneity systematically distributed across the field, or tracking errors. Investigate before trusting.

High R² + steep slope

Strong, well-defined spatial relationship. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) doesn't guarantee biological relevance, but a high R² suggests distance is a real predictor of correlation.

Low R² + significant p

With many pairs, even tiny slopes are statistically significant. p < 0.05 with R² ≈ 0.01 means: there is a trend, but distance explains 1% of the variance. Don't over-interpret.

Pooled vs per-channel

If channels disagree on slope direction, pooling can wash out the signal. The per-channel figure shows whether the trend is consistent. The combined figure is misleading if channels differ.

Watch out

Pitfalls

What this figure quietly assumes

Cells don't move much. "Mean position" averages (x, y) over all frames. If cells migrate substantially, the mean is a poor representation of where the cell was at any given time. Distances between mean positions become approximate.

Per-cluster panels need clustering.py to have run first. {ch}_corr_vs_dist_c{k}.png reads bg_cache/clusters/<exp>__<ch>.pkl. If clustering hasn't run for that channel, only the per-channel and combined figures appear.

Pearson and Spearman tell different stories. Spearman is robust to monotonic non-linearities; Pearson responds to amplitude alignment. If the two rows disagree on slope sign, the relationship is rank-driven rather than linear. Inspect both before drawing conclusions.

Pairwise NaN handling. pandas .corr() uses pairwise deletion by default. Cells with very different frame coverage produce r values based on different denominators, biasing the comparison.

SEM band width. ±3 SEM is a 99.7% confidence band on the fitted line, not on individual scatter points. Visually it can look tight even when the scatter is wide. R² (variance explained) is the truer measure of fit tightness.

Distance is in pixels. If you want micrometers, multiply by the scope's pixel-size calibration (not done here).
